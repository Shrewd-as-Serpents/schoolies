## Introduction

Did you know that a book arguing that Native Americans were subjected to a genocide can be classified in library catalogs under "Christopher Columbus—Influence" or "Economic Policy," completely hiding it from researchers of mass atrocities? Who controls language controls how we interpret the past. This lesson explores how Western systems of knowledge organization—from library catalogs to literary genres—can actively sanitize, minimize, or erase historical genocides, and how scholars and writers are fighting back.

By completing this lesson, you will be able to:

* Analyze how Western knowledge organization systems, like the Library of Congress Subject Headings, marginalize histories of Indigenous genocides.
* Explain "genre trouble" and how first-person *testimonio* narratives disrupt linear, colonial chronopolitics.

* Identify strategies for decolonizing history in both library science and literary representation.

## The Power of Naming: How Libraries Classify Atrocities

For over a century, libraries have relied on classification systems like the Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH) to organize human knowledge. While these systems claim to be neutral and objective, critics argue they are deeply Eurocentric, reflecting the biases of the "Established Order" and reproducing colonial power dynamics.

One of the most stark examples of this bias is the euphemistic way the library describes colonial violence. While the Jewish Holocaust, the Rwandan Genocide, and the Holodomor in Ukraine are cataloged with unambiguous terms like "Genocide" or "Holocaust," books on the systemic destruction of Native American populations are frequently stripped of this terminology. Instead, they are assigned passive or neutral headings like "Indians, Treatment of" or "Government relations."

This discrepancy is not a minor indexing error; it has material consequences. When catalogers do not assign the subject heading "Genocide" to books that explicitly argue a genocide occurred, they violate their own professional principle of "literary warrant" (cataloging books based on what authors actually write). More importantly, they create a major barrier to discovery. A researcher searching the database for "Genocide—United States" will miss nearly 60% of the relevant books simply because those books were buried under sanitized headings.

By hiding these historical realities, the library functions as a "colonial node of governance," reinforcing a broader cultural narrative of Native American Holocaust denial.

## Decolonizing Time and Genre: Testimonio and Genre Trouble

Just as libraries classify books, literary academies classify writing into strict genres. This division also has colonial roots. Traditional Western narratives of migration, for instance, often rely on a linear "chronopolitics"—a developmental timeline that portrays marginalized, colonized spaces as "primitive" or "stuck in the past," while Western nations represent the modern "present."

To challenge this linear temporal mapping, Latin American and Caribbean writers in the mid-to-late 20th century popularized the *testimonio* genre. A *testimonio* is a first-person narrative where the narrator speaks not just for themselves, but as an agent of a collective struggle, denouncing state exploitation, military violence, or genocide.

However, the *testimonio* has faced its own colonial policing. In the late 1990s, the "Rigoberta Menchú controversy" erupted when Western anthropologists attacked the Nobel laureate's testimony for containing "inaccuracies." These critics demanded strict, Western standards of legalistic proof, dismissing the collective, oral traditions of the Maya people.

In response, writers like Edwidge Danticat utilize "genre trouble"—a concept adapted from Judith Butler's "gender trouble"—to decolonize the narrative. In her novel *The Farming of Bones*, which details the 1937 Trujillo genocide of Haitian workers in the Dominican Republic, Danticat intentionally blurs the lines between fiction, personal memoir, history, and ethnography. By refusing to conform to a single, easily categorized Western genre, the text resists academic capture and forces readers to confront the ongoing political realities of the genocide, rather than viewing it as a closed chapter of the "obsolete" past.

## Language as a Biopolitical Weapon: The Differend and the Perejil Test

How does language transition from an abstract classification system into a literal matter of life and death? During the 1937 massacre of Haitian migrants by the Dominican dictatorship of Rafael Trujillo, language was weaponized as a tool of biopolitical surveillance.

To identify Haitian workers fleeing across the border, Dominican soldiers implemented the *perejil* (parsley) test. Soldiers would hold up a sprig of parsley and demand that people name it. Those who could trill the "r" in the Spanish word *perejil* were let go; those whose French-inflected Creole accent betrayed them were executed on the spot. Here, language became a literal border, where pronunciation dictated survival.

This horrific historical event illustrates the French philosopher Jean-François Lyotard’s concept of the *differend*. A *differend* occurs when a conflict is regulated entirely in the language and legal idiom of one dominant party, making it impossible for the victim to signify the wrong they have suffered in their own terms.

We see the *differend* play out in the aftermath of the massacre. When survivors attempted to testify before state officials and international human rights bodies, they were told they had to bring "papers" and "proof" that their families had died. If they did testify, their oral narratives were transcribed and translated into legalistic, bureaucratic jargon. As one survivor in Danticat's novel puts it: "You testify, and then they retell your story in their way, in words that you will not understand, in a language that is theirs, not yours."

To decolonize history, we must recognize these linguistic trapdoors and allow marginalized survivors to speak on their own terms.

## Summary

Language and organization are never neutral; they are deeply entangled with power, sovereignty, and memory. Here are the key takeaways from this lesson:

* **Library Erasure:** Library classification systems often use euphemistic language (like "Treatment of" instead of "Genocide") to catalog Indigenous encounters, creating barrier to discovery and academic study.
* **Chronopolitics:** Linear time frameworks portray colonized peoples as "primitive" or "stuck in the past" to justify ongoing developmental intervention.
* **Genre Trouble:** Blurring the lines between fiction and historical testimony allows writers to resist Western academic policing and preserve collective memory.
* **The** **Differend** **:** True justice is often blocked when victims are forced to articulate their trauma entirely within the language, legal frameworks, and cataloging systems of their oppressors.
